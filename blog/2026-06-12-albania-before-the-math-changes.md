12.4 million people visited Albania in 2025. That's nearly double the pre-pandemic count, in a country of under three million people. In Gjirokastra — an Ottoman stone city in the southern mountains — visitor numbers have increased sixfold since 2019. And you can still get a full dinner with wine in Saranda for under €10.

That last sentence has an expiration date on it.

Every destination that reaches this inflection point goes one of two ways: infrastructure catches up faster than prices, or prices catch up faster than infrastructure. Albania is currently in the gap between those two outcomes — wide enough to walk through, narrowing visibly. This is not a "discover it before anyone else does" pitch. Plenty of people are already there. It's more specific than that: go before the pricing logic that makes Albania extraordinary stops making sense.

---

Albania is two countries, essentially. There's the interior — Tirana, which is genuinely worth a couple of days, and Gjirokastra, which is something else entirely — and there's the coast: 440 kilometers of Ionian Sea that shares the same water as the Greek islands directly across from it, at roughly a third of the cost.

Start in Tirana because the airport is there, and because skipping Tirana means missing something. The city rebuilt itself in a hurry after the regime fell in 1991 and the results are unpredictable in the best way — socialist concrete blocks next to pastel-painted facades next to actual modernist architecture. Head for Blloku. During the communist era, this neighborhood was a restricted zone for Party officials. Now it's the city's bar district, packed with outdoor terraces, alive until 3am, and priced like a city that knows it still has something to prove. Nouvelle Vague was ranked the 27th best bar in the world by the World's 50 Best Bars in 2025. A cocktail there will cost you €6. Era Blloku on Rruga Ismail Qemali has been serving Albanian food since 1999 — byrek, tave kosi (yogurt-baked lamb), proper grilled meats. The whole bill for two is probably €20.

---

Then go south. Gjirokastra is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, which you'd know immediately from the architecture — slate-roofed stone houses climbing a hillside, the 12th-century fortress sitting above the valley like it's been there since before the concept of tourism was invented. It has. The castle contains, among other things, a captured American U-2 spy plane from 1957, which the communist government shot down and kept as a trophy. The Cold War Tunnel below the city is a genuine bunker — dug into the mountainside under Enver Hoxha's orders, opened to visitors only after 2012, and still a bit surreal to walk through. For a traditional meal, Tradicional Restaurant Odaja serves Albanian food at the kind of prices that make you double-check the bill.

![Zekate House, Gjirokastra — an example of the Ottoman tower-house architecture that defines the old city](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8b/Gjirokastër_–_Zekate_House_01_–_front.jpg)

Six times the visitors as 2019, but Gjirokastra is not yet insufferable. The crowds have not yet reached the density where you're queuing for cobblestones.

---

From Gjirokastra, the coast is 90 minutes south. Saranda sits at the bottom of Albania on the Ionian Sea, with Corfu visibly across the water. Join the evening xhiro — the Albanian tradition of the post-dinner stroll — along Hasan Tahsini Boulevard when the promenade fills with locals and the light over the harbor goes orange. Lekursi Castle, up on the hill above the city, offers the best vantage point over the coast and Corfu if you get there before dark.

Ksamil is a 20-minute bus ride south of Saranda, and it's what you've probably already seen on someone's Instagram. Turquoise water, white pebbles, small islets you can kayak to. The "Maldives of Europe" label that gets attached to it is embarrassing and also not entirely wrong. The water is that color. It's genuinely that clear. The ancient ruins of Butrint are just a few kilometers away — Greek, then Roman, then Byzantine, now a national park — and most visitors to Ksamil skip them entirely, which means you probably don't have to.

---

Dhermi is where the Riviera gets more interesting. It sits on the coast below the Ceraunian Mountains, 5 kilometers of white pebbles and water that's the specific shade of blue that makes you understand why people keep coming to the Mediterranean specifically. It's more local than Ksamil, less organized as a tourist destination, and probably the better version of the Albanian beach experience if you're not primarily there to post photos. The mountain drive in — through Llogara Pass — is one of the more dramatic approaches to any beach town in Europe.

![Dhermi beach on the Albanian Riviera](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1a/Dhërmi_plazh.JPG)

---

The math that makes all of this work: Albania's pricing is currently 40-60% below comparable destinations in Greece, Croatia, or Montenegro. Meals, accommodation, transport — all of it. That gap has been narrowing. New hotel infrastructure is going in. Airlines are adding routes. The EU accession process is ongoing, and when that changes, so will the currency dynamics and eventually the prices.

None of this happens overnight. Albania is not about to become Dubrovnik by August. But the trajectory is obvious, and the question is just how many seasons remain before the specific combination of quality and affordability that defines the place right now becomes a story you tell about somewhere you went.

---

One honest thing: the road to the Riviera from Tirana goes through Llogara Pass. It's narrow, steep, single-lane in several places, and spectacular. It is not a motorway. Do not rent a car and assume otherwise. Hire a driver in Tirana, or take a furgon — the shared minibuses that run between cities and work on informal departure schedules that basically mean "when it's full." The furgon costs almost nothing and the driver will have navigated that pass a thousand times. You haven't.
