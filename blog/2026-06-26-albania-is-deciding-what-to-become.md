Albania is in the streets right now. Not metaphorically — literally. For weeks, tens of thousands of Albanians have been demonstrating against a $1.6 billion luxury resort planned for Sazan Island, a protected marine national park. The protests grew into something bigger: the largest anti-government demonstrations in years, about corruption, transparency, and who gets to decide what happens to Albania's coastline.

That is the travel angle. Not the resort. The question the protests are asking.

Go now, while the answer isn't settled.

## What Albania Actually Is

A small country of three million people on the Adriatic coast, sandwiched between North Macedonia, Kosovo, Montenegro, and Greece. It spent 40 years under one of the most paranoid communist dictatorships in history — more isolated than North Korea in certain respects, points some historians make without exaggeration. Enver Hoxha built 700,000 concrete bunkers across the country. One for every four citizens. You still see them everywhere: in fields, on beaches, half-buried in hillsides.

The beaches are some of the best in the Mediterranean — genuinely turquoise, not photographically enhanced turquoise. The tavë kosi (baked lamb with egg and yogurt) is worth making a trip for on its own. It is 30 to 50 percent cheaper than Greece or Croatia next door. Albania recorded 11.7 million visitors in 2024 — 80 percent above pre-pandemic numbers — and it still hasn't been ruined.

That combination won't last.

## Four Places Worth Your Time

![Ksamil beach on the Albanian Riviera](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/67/Ksamil_albanian_riviera.jpg)

**Ksamil.** Four beaches at the southern tip of the Riviera, turquoise water, small islands you can swim to, and the Roman ruins of UNESCO-listed Butrint ten minutes away. This is the stretch that development money is most directly targeting — the postcard image that attracts investors — and it still feels like a beach town that belongs to itself. Arrive before noon in July and August. Bring cash. Expect to pay for an umbrella and chairs at the best spots; the umbrella mafia has arrived, though it's still negotiable.

**Berat.** UNESCO-listed and actually worth it, which isn't a given. The old town climbs a hillside, Ottoman white houses stacked densely, each with so many windows that from below it looks like the whole city is watching you. It's called "the city of a thousand windows." Antigoni Restaurant in the castle quarter does the classics without pretension — stuffed peppers, grilled lamb, fresh local wine. Locals eat there. That's the signal that matters.

![Old town of Berat, Albania, with its stacked Ottoman houses](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/33/Street_in_Berat,_Albania.jpg)

**Tirana, specifically Blloku.** From 1944 to 1991, the Blloku district was walled off for the communist elite. Hoxha's villa is still there. Today the neighborhood is the city's social center: cafes and wine bars packed onto every block, outdoor tables spilling onto cobblestones. A macchiato costs under one euro at any neighborhood place. Mullixhiu, a fifteen-minute walk toward the Grand Park, does a seven-course tasting menu for €30 — farm-to-table Albanian cooking that doesn't perform rusticity, it just is it. Book ahead, because it fills up.

**The Pyramid.** Built as Hoxha's mausoleum in 1988. Never used as planned. Repurposed, nearly demolished, fought over for years, and now home to TUMO Tirana — a youth technology and creative education center. You can still climb the brutalist concrete slopes on the outside. The interior is full of teenagers working on animation and music production. It is one of the stranger things you will encounter in any European capital, and Tirana is proud of it in a complicated, accurate way.

## Why Go This Year Specifically

The protests are about one resort, but they're also about the terms on which Albania develops. If that deal goes through, similar projects will follow. The Riviera as it exists right now — accessible, ungated, priced for actual humans — is on a clock. Ksamil in particular will transform. Five years is a generous estimate.

EU accession is not imminent (full membership is still a decade out at minimum), but the infrastructure investment is arriving now. Roads are being paved. New hotels are opening. The gap between "undiscovered" and "discovered" closes fast, and Albania is deep into that transition.

The country is also politically loud right now, which makes it interesting as a destination in a way that has nothing to do with political tourism. The debate about what it should become is happening out loud, in public, with stakes you can see.

## The Honest Caveat

The drive to Dhërmi on the southern Riviera is a mountain road with genuinely alarming switchbacks and views worth pulling over for. Albanian is not a language that maps, menus, or instinct help much with — bring patience for uncertainty, because some of it is actually enjoyable. Summers in the south are properly hot, and the best beaches are crowded in July and August for good reason. September is the answer if you have flexibility.

The protests have been peaceful and are concentrated in Tirana; tourist areas are not affected. But the political context is real, the government's response is being scrutinized by the EU, and the country is genuinely in flux.

That's not a reason to avoid it. It's why going now is different from going later.
