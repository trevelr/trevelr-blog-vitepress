Sixteen cities across three countries are hosting this World Cup, and four of them — Mexico City, New York/New Jersey, Los Angeles, Miami — don't need my help. Everyone already knows those are worth the flight. This list is the other twelve, minus two I cut for being redundant sprawl (sorry, Houston and the Bay Area), ranked by a single question: if your team goes out in the group stage and you're stuck with three unplanned days, how good is the city underneath the tournament? Access, cost, and whether there's a real reason to be there beyond the 90 minutes all count. One pick here is going to make Toronto and Vancouver fans furious.

## #10 — Dallas (AT&T Stadium, Arlington)

**The stadium isn't even in Dallas, and that tells you most of what you need to know.** AT&T Stadium sits in Arlington, roughly equidistant from Dallas and Fort Worth and walkable from neither — you're renting a car or you're at the mercy of shuttle buses. What redeems the trip is Oak Cliff's Bishop Arts District and the specific, non-negotiable pilgrimage to the Sixth Floor Museum at Dealey Plaza, which does more with a single floor of exhibits than most city museums manage in ten.

*Caveat:* This is a semifinal venue with a Dallas name and a 25-minute commute reality. Budget for it.

## #9 — Boston

**Compact, walkable, and completely disconnected from its own stadium.** Gillette Stadium is 30 miles south in Foxborough, and outside of a special game-day MBTA layover service, getting there without a car is a genuine project. The city itself is the payoff: Fenway, the Freedom Trail done in an afternoon without irony, and a density that means you can actually build a full day on foot between matches.

*Caveat:* Everyone's already been to Boston. It's a great World Cup city, not a discovery.

## #8 — Philadelphia

**The most underrated food city in the country happens to be hosting six matches.** Reading Terminal Market and the Italian Market in South Philly do more for a three-day trip than any single attraction on this list, and Lincoln Financial Field is actually in the city — a straight shot down the Broad Street subway line, no shuttle bus negotiation required.

*Caveat:* Philly's sports crowd reputation is not exaggerated. If you're traveling with a rival jersey on, expect noise.

## #7 — Atlanta

**One of two semifinal hosts, and the only one where the walk from downtown to the stadium is pleasant.** Mercedes-Benz Stadium sits blocks from the Beltline, Atlanta's converted-rail walking and biking loop that strings together a half-dozen neighborhoods and the Buford Highway food corridor — one of the best international food strips in the US, full stop.

*Caveat:* That Beltline core is an island. Step outside it and Atlanta reverts to the car-dependent sprawl it's known for, and July humidity here is genuinely punishing.

## #6 — Guadalajara

**The only Mexican host city outside the capital, and the one where match days come with a built-in day trip.** Estadio Akron sits well outside the historic center, but that center — the cathedral, Teatro Degollado, the UNESCO-listed Hospicio Cabañas — is worth the trip on its own, and the Tequila Express train runs day visitors 35 miles out to actual agave country and 250-year-old distilleries.

*Caveat:* June and July heat in Jalisco is real, and the stadium-to-center commute eats a chunk of any match day.

## #5 — Toronto

**Canada's larger host city, and the one that rewards wandering more than sightseeing.** BMO Field is a short streetcar ride from downtown, and the city's actual strength is neighborhood-level food — Kensington Market, the various immigrant-community strips — rather than a marquee attraction list. It's also, unusually for this tournament, a city where you can do the whole trip without a car.

*Caveat:* Toronto is expensive even by North American standards, and BMO Field's modest capacity (topped up with temporary seating for the tournament) means it's a lower-key World Cup experience than the other Canadian venue.

## #4 — Vancouver

**Mountains, ocean, and a downtown stadium, which is a combination nothing else on this list offers.** BC Place sits in the middle of the city; Stanley Park's seawall is a legitimate half-day bike ride; Whistler and Grouse Mountain are close enough for a genuine side trip if your team is out early.

*Caveat:* Hotel prices during the tournament are going to be absurd, and BC Place's $180 million in renovations were specifically about capacity — meaning a lot of visitors will be watching from fan zones, not seats.

## #3 — Seattle

**The best airport-to-stadium connection in the entire tournament, and June–July is objectively the best three weeks of Seattle weather all year.** The Link light rail runs from Sea-Tac directly to Stadium Station in 40 minutes for $3.75 — no other host city makes it this easy — and Lumen Field sits within walking distance of Pioneer Square and the waterfront. Pair a match with a day trip to Mount Rainier or the Olympic Peninsula.

*Caveat:* Everything outside the city core requires a full day, not an afternoon. Don't plan Rainier and a market visit for the same day.

## #2 — Monterrey

**The most beautiful backdrop in the tournament, and most people can't name the city it's in.** Estadio BBVA — nicknamed the Steel Giant — opens on its north side to a direct view of Cerro de la Silla, a 1,820-meter peak that makes every wide shot from this stadium look staged. Off match days: Parque Fundidora's Horno 3 steel museum (climb the old blast furnace for a 360-degree view), and La Huasteca canyon and Chipinque for hiking and rock climbing within city limits — genuinely rare for a metro of five million people.

*Caveat:* Monterrey doesn't have Mexico City or Guadalajara's tourist infrastructure — less English signage, fewer set-piece attractions — and it carries a safety reputation from the 2010s that's outdated but still shapes how people plan around it. Check current guidance before you book.

## #1 — Kansas City

**The most controversial pick on this list, and I will defend it against Vancouver, Toronto, and Mexico City if you make me.** Arrowhead Stadium holds the record for the loudest crowd roar ever measured at an outdoor sporting event, which tells you something about what a match day here sounds like before you even get to the tailgate lots — genuinely the best in American sports, and the reason Kansas City knows how to host without local infrastructure choking on itself. Connect KC 26, a purpose-built network of 215 motorcoaches running every 15–20 minutes from 15 regional hubs, solves the exact access problem that dogs Dallas and Boston. The Crossroads Arts District delivers murals, breweries, and boutique shopping in a genuinely walkable few blocks, and the cost of doing all of it is a fraction of what the coastal host cities charge.

*Caveat:* Kansas City's non-football, non-BBQ attraction list runs thin. Three days here is close to the right amount — this is the value pick on the list, not the depth pick, and I'd rank it behind Monterrey the moment the tournament ends and the transit system goes back to being ordinary.
