In 2019, Ghana ran a marketing campaign called Year of Return, timed to the 400th anniversary of the first documented slave ship landing in Virginia. It worked better than anyone in the tourism ministry probably expected — record arrivals, global press, a visible shift in how the African diaspora talked about "going home." Most countries would let a moment like that pass. Ghana turned it into a government program: Beyond the Return, now in its sixth year, with a citizenship track, a Right of Abode framework for diaspora applicants, and — as of this year — a four-year, GH¢4.35 billion plan targeting 70% growth in international arrivals by 2029. Delta added a second US route. United still flies Dulles–Accra nonstop.

That is a lot of institutional weight to put behind a tourism circuit built around a former slave-trading fort. Which is exactly the tension worth writing about.

## What's Actually Happening

Ghana isn't selling a beach destination with a heritage add-on. It's selling the heritage, full stop, and building the rest of the trip around it. The itinerary almost everyone follows is the same one it was in 2019: two or three days in Accra, then a drive to the Central Region coast to see Cape Coast Castle and Elmina Castle, the two UNESCO-listed forts where captives were held before being shipped across the Atlantic. What's changed is the scale of the machinery around that itinerary — official ceremonies, investment pathways, a tourism ministry treating heritage travel as the primary growth lever for the whole sector, not a niche within it.

Accra itself splits cleanly. Osu is where the money and the nightlife are — European and Ghanaian fusion menus, sea-view restaurants, the kind of city block that could be Lagos or Nairobi or anywhere else with a rising middle class. Jamestown, twenty minutes away, is the older Accra: a Ga fishing community that's been hauling canoes onto the same stretch of sand since before the British built a fort there. That's where the actual texture of the city is.

## Four Places That Explain It

**Jamestown Fishing Harbour.** Go at sunrise if you can manage it. Wooden canoes painted in blues and yellows come in with the morning catch, nets get mended on the sand, and the whole thing runs on rhythms that predate the tourism ministry by centuries. Jamestown Coffee Co., a few blocks up, roasts beans from the Volta Region and serves a Labadi iced latte that has nothing to do with the neighborhood's fishing economy and everything to do with what happens when a place gets discovered — a small, honest example of Accra absorbing new money without becoming unrecognizable, at least so far.

![Fishing canoes at Jamestown Harbour, Accra — the working district the heritage circuit passes through](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d5/Jamestown_Fishing_Harbour_(22124184491).jpg)

**Cape Coast Castle.** Built by the Swedish in 1653, seized by the British, and operated as a slave-trading post for two centuries — this is the site everything else is organized around. The tour ends at the Door of No Return, exactly as heavy as that name suggests. It's also where the tension I opened with is sharpest: scholars and diaspora visitors have flagged for years that guides solicit donations at a spiritual altar inside the dungeon itself, and that repeated tour framing has, for some visitors, started to feel less like a memorial and more like a stop on a circuit. That criticism isn't new, but an $8.3 billion growth plan makes it more urgent, not less.

**Elmina Castle.** Fifteen minutes down the coast and older by 171 years — the Portuguese built it in 1482, which makes it the oldest surviving European structure in Sub-Saharan Africa. Go here after Cape Coast Castle, not instead of it. The two sites tell different parts of the same story: Portuguese trading post that predates the transatlantic slave trade by decades, versus British-run fort built specifically for it.

![Elmina Castle, built by the Portuguese in 1482 — the older, quieter counterpart to Cape Coast Castle](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a7/Elmina_Castle_-_Ghana.jpg)

**Kakum National Park.** The one stop on the circuit that isn't about history. The canopy walkway runs 330 meters across seven rope bridges, 40 meters above the rainforest floor, and it's the closest thing to a decompression the itinerary offers after two castles in one day. Go early — the walkway sways more than you'd like, and the heat by midday makes the climb up to it miserable.

![The Kakum canopy walkway, 40 meters above the rainforest — the itinerary's one break from history](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/95/Kakum_Canopy_Walk,_Kakum_National_Park,_Ghana.JPG)

## Why Go Now

Because the infrastructure question is being answered in real time. Two nonstop US routes into Accra didn't exist a few years ago. The government's four-year investment plan just opened this year. Beyond the Return has moved past ceremony into actual policy — citizenship, land, investment access for diaspora applicants who want to stay longer than a week. This is the window where Ghana is still building the thing, not finished selling a polished version of it. What that looks like in 2029, once the growth targets either land or don't, is a different trip.

## The Honest Caveat

Cape Coast Castle is not a place you visit lightly, and the commercialization criticism is legitimate — a site built to hold the worst thing a place can memorialize should not also feel like it's working an angle. Go in prepared for that friction rather than surprised by it. And be clear-eyed about logistics: the Cape Coast circuit is a three-to-four-hour drive each way from Accra on roads that don't always match the pace of the marketing. Budget a full day, not an afternoon.
