Search interest in Sint Maarten is up 133% year over year, the fastest-rising number of any Caribbean destination this summer. The obvious explanation — new direct flights — is real but incomplete. The more interesting number is this: roughly 80% of the island's visitors arrive by cruise ship and never spend a night. More than a million people came through in the first three months of 2026 alone, and most of them saw the island for four hours between excursions and a dinner reservation somewhere else.

That's the actual story. A destination is trending and almost nobody who visits it is staying long enough to find out why.

## What Sint Maarten Actually Is

It's one island, 34 square miles, split into two countries with no border checkpoint between them. Sint Maarten is the Dutch side — Princess Juliana Airport, Philipsburg, the cruise port. Saint Martin is the French side — Marigot, Grand Case, and a coastline that looks nothing like the postcard version of the Dutch half. You can drive from one to the other in fifteen minutes and the only sign you crossed is the language on the road signs and what currency the register wants.

Southwest started flying nonstop from Orlando this year, and added a second route from Baltimore/Washington — the airline's first-ever service to the island. That's the concrete reason behind the search spike: for a chunk of the East Coast and Midwest, this stopped requiring a connection through San Juan. Getting here got boring in the way that actually matters for trip planning.

## Four Places That Aren't the Cruise Dock

**Maho Beach**, on the Dutch side, is the plane-spotting spot — jets on final approach into Princess Juliana pass low enough over the sand that people brace against the jet blast for photos. It's genuinely something to see once. It's also the single most photographed and least representative spot on the island, packed with people who never make it past the beach bar with the flight tracker screen. Go for twenty minutes, then leave.

![Low approach into Princess Juliana Airport over Maho Beach](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b4/Maho_Beach,_near_Princess_Juliana_Airport,_Caribbean_island_of_Saint_Martin-8Feb2008_(3).jpg)

**Marigot Market**, on the French side, runs Wednesday and Saturday mornings along the waterfront near Fort Louis. Fish, conch, and produce stalls next to Creole food huts selling stew chicken and garlic shrimp for cash. This is the version of the island that isn't performing for anyone — it's just the Wednesday market, and it happens whether or not a cruise ship is in port that day.

![Marigot Market on the waterfront, Wednesday and Saturday mornings](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f1/Marigot_Market_(16278947766).jpg)

**Grand Case**, a mile-long strip of restaurants on the island's northern tip, is the reason people call the French side the culinary capital of the Caribbean. The move is to skip the white-tablecloth places with the sea views and eat at a lolo instead — the open-air barbecue stands lining the beach that grill fish and ribs over oil-drum smokers for a fraction of the price. Order the mahi. Sit on plastic furniture. It's better than the restaurant next door charging four times as much for the same fish.

![Grand Case, mile-long restaurant strip on Saint Martin's northern coast](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/15/Grand_Case.JPG)

**Simpson Bay Lagoon**, the enclosed body of water separating the two coasts, is where the island actually functions — fishing boats, yacht moorings, the causeway bridge that opens on a schedule to let sailboats through. It's not a beach and it's not trying to be scenic. It's the working middle of the island, and seeing it from above makes the geography of the whole "two countries, one landmass" arrangement click in a way a map doesn't.

![Simpson Bay Lagoon separates the Dutch and French coasts](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9b/Simpson_Baie_Lagoon_and_Marigot_from_above_-_panoramio.jpg)

## Why Go Now

Hurricane Irma hit in 2017. Nine years later, the rebuild is largely finished — Princess Juliana reopened with hurricane-resilient jet bridges and a $72 million reconstruction fund behind it, and the hotel stock has mostly caught up. That matters because for a long stretch after 2017, "is it actually open" was a legitimate question for this island. It isn't anymore, but the perception lag hasn't fully closed, which is part of why it still reads as underrated even with a 133% search jump.

Combine that with the new direct routes and you get a short window: infrastructure that's caught up, flights that finally make sense, and a search trend that hasn't yet translated into the kind of crowds that make a place stop being itself. Zip-lining over the interior hills is reportedly the fastest-growing activity search tied to the island right now — a sign people are starting to look past the beach chair, which is a good sign for how this trends next.

## The Honest Caveat

The cruise-day math is real and it will shape your trip whether you like it or not. Philipsburg's Front Street turns into a duty-free shopping gauntlet whenever ships are in port, which is most days. If you're staying overnight, structure around it: do Philipsburg early morning or skip it, and spend the bulk of your time on the French side, where the rhythm doesn't reset every few hours around a docking schedule.

And Maho Beach's fame is doing a lot of unearned work. It's worth the visit, but if a "trending destination" write-up shows you nothing but a photo of a jet buzzing sunbathers, that's the whole island reduced to one loud postcard. The market, the lolos, and the lagoon are where it actually is.

I did the research. I haven't been. Go find out what I missed.
