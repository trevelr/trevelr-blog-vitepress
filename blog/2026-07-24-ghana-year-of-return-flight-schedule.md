In 2019, Ghana's government launched "Year of Return" — 400 years since the first recorded enslaved Africans were forced onto a ship bound for Virginia, and an open invitation to the African diaspora to come back, invest, stay. Governments announce things like this constantly. Most get a press release and a fade-out. This one didn't fade.

This month, Emirates flew its first Boeing 777-300ER into Kotoka International Airport, taking Accra to 11 weekly flights on the route. That's not a marketing decision. Airlines don't reroute widebody aircraft because a hashtag did well one summer — they do it when a demand curve holds for seven consecutive years and the forecasting models stop treating it as a fluke. The campaign ended in December 2019. The flights kept getting added anyway. That's the story: not that Ghana launched a heritage tourism initiative, but that the initiative outlived its own marketing budget and became infrastructure.

## What Accra Actually Is

Accra is a coastal city of roughly 2.5 million people that was never built to be a pilgrimage site, and it shows in the best way — nobody's performing heritage for you here. It's doing its own thing: Afrobeats blasting out of trotro minibuses, a fashion and design scene that's been quietly exporting to London and New York for a decade, jollof rice arguments that will outlast every government currently in power. The diaspora tourism is real and it's growing, but it's layered on top of a functioning city, not built to replace one.

The heritage sites that anchor the "return" narrative — the slave castles at Cape Coast and Elmina — are two to three hours west along the coast, not in Accra itself. That drive matters. It's the difference between a long weekend and a trip that actually reckons with what you came for.

## Four Places That Matter

**Oxford Street, Osu.** This is the closest thing Accra has to a single defining strip — roughly a kilometer of bars, hair salons, phone-repair stalls, and restaurants that gets loud on Friday nights in a way that feels earned rather than staged for visitors. Firefly Bar has outdoor seating and a crowd that's genuinely mixed, expats and locals and diaspora returnees all working through the same beer list. Order kelewele — spiced fried plantain — from a street vendor before you sit down anywhere. It's better than most of what's on the menus.

**Jamestown.** The oldest part of the city, and the one that's changed the least on the surface — Fort James still stands, the 1930s lighthouse still works, and the fishing canoes still come in at dawn the way they have for generations. What's new is the street art: a biennial Chale Wote festival turned the neighborhood's colonial-era walls into a rotating open-air gallery, and it's stuck. Go for the fish. Grilled tilapia and banku from a chop bar near the harbor, eaten with your hands, is a better introduction to Ga food than anything in a hotel restaurant.

![Jamestown's Mantse Palace, in the oldest part of Accra](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d6/Jamestown_Mantse_Palace_(Accra,_Ghana_2017).jpg)

**Cape Coast Castle.** This is the site people are actually flying in for, whether they say so or not — a whitewashed fort where enslaved people were held before being forced through the "Door of No Return" onto ships. The dungeons are unrenovated on purpose. Walking through them is not a pleasant afternoon activity, and it isn't supposed to be.

![Cape Coast Castle, on Ghana's central coast](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a0/Cape_Coast_Castle,_Cape_Coast,_Ghana.JPG)

**Kakum National Park.** Forty minutes inland from Cape Coast, seven suspension bridges strung 130 feet up in the rainforest canopy, built in 1995 and still one of only three canopy walks on the continent. Go at dawn if you can manage it — the crowds thin out and the forest is loud with hornbills instead of tour groups.

![The canopy walkway at Kakum National Park](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/95/Kakum_Canopy_Walk,_Kakum_National_Park,_Ghana.JPG)

## Why Go Now

Because the flight math changed this year, not seven years ago. More seats means lower fares and easier routing, and that window — after the connectivity improves but before the itinerary gets fully packaged into a $4,000 "heritage tour" — tends to close fast. Ghana's tourism authority is explicitly chasing higher-value, longer-stay visitors for 2026, which is the polite way of saying prices are heading up.

## The Honest Caveat

The castles are becoming a business, and not everyone is comfortable with how. In 2019, workers bulldozed ground directly in front of Elmina Castle to lay a parking lot — a literal paving-over of the approach to a mass grave. Diaspora visitors have described feeling anger at how commercialized the experience has gotten, and some Ghanaians have pushed back on the idea that a nation profiting from slave-trade tourism gets to sidestep its own historical complicity in that trade. There's no clean way through this. Go anyway, but go aware that you're walking into an argument, not just a monument.
