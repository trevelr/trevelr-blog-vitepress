Shuri Castle burned to the ground on October 31, 2019. Not damaged — gone. The main hall, the ceremonial buildings, 421 artifacts, roughly 4,800 square meters of Ryukyu Kingdom history, reduced to a scorched foundation on live television while a whole country watched. Third time in the castle's history it had burned. Japan is rebuilding it anyway, with the same lacquer, joinery, and roof-tile methods the original 15th-century builders used, at a cost of about 7.3 billion yen. Completion ceremony: November 22. Public opening: the next day.

That's the hook, but not the whole story. Expedia just named Okinawa its number two global destination for 2026, off a 71% spike in searches. The New York Times put it on its 52 Places list. The pitch making the rounds is "budget Hawaii" — same reef, same heat, a fraction of the price. Okinawa is about to get discovered twice in the same year, by two different audiences, for two different reasons. Right now, for about four more months, you can see the castle mid-rebuild and the island before the budget-Hawaii crowd fully lands. That overlap is the story.

## What Okinawa Actually Is

Not mainland Japan with better weather. Okinawa was its own kingdom — the Ryukyu Kingdom — until Japan annexed it in 1879, and the residue of that independence is everywhere: its own dialect, its own cuisine, its own relationship to ancestor worship that mainland Japanese tourists find just as foreign as anyone else does. Then came 1945, the bloodiest ground battle of the Pacific war, followed by 27 years of direct US administration and a military footprint that never fully left — roughly a third of the main island is still occupied by American bases. You can't understand the place without that layered history. You'll see it in the fences, in the food, in the fact that "American Village" is a real neighborhood name, not a joke.

## Shuri Castle

Go now specifically because it's unfinished. The Seiden's exterior came out from behind scaffolding in July 2025, so you can see the roofline and the vermilion lacquer work in progress before the interior opens to the public this November. Watching a UNESCO site get rebuilt with hand tools beats showing up after it's a finished museum piece — the workmanship is the exhibit right now. Once the doors open November 23, expect the crowds that a 71% search spike implies.

## Tsuboya and Makishi Market, Naha

South of Kokusai-dori, Naha's main tourist drag, Tsuboya is a narrow, sloped stone street that's been the center of Okinawan pottery since the 17th century, when the Ryukyu Kingdom forced every kiln on the island to consolidate here. It still works: small shops selling shisa lion-dog figures and glazed bowls, kilns you can see from the street, and the Tsuboya Pottery Museum for ¥350 if you want the history instead of just the souvenirs. Five minutes away, the Makishi Public Market — locals call it Naha's kitchen — was torn down and rebuilt in 2023, and the new building is doing exactly what the old one did: fish you point at downstairs, cooked upstairs, for the price of the fish plus a small fee. Skip the taxi and walk between the two; that walk is the neighborhood.

## American Village, Chatan

This is the base-culture layer made literal. American Village sits on land a US military base occupied until the late 1990s, when it was returned and redeveloped into a boardwalk of surf shops, a Ferris wheel, and a sunset over the East China Sea that locals treat as a legitimate evening plan, not a tourist trap. It reads as kitschy until you clock what it actually represents — reclaimed base land turned into something the town chose for itself. For dinner, don't overthink it: get taco rice, the ground-beef-lettuce-tomato-on-rice dish invented in 1984 at a restaurant near Camp Hansen for American GIs who wanted tacos without shells. The original recipe survives at King Tacos in Kin Town, but you'll find honest versions at half the diners in Chatan.

## Kerama Islands

A fifty-minute ferry from Tomari Port in Naha gets you to Zamami, the gateway to the Kerama Islands — a national park with more than 200 coral species and water clear enough that "the Kerama Blue" is an actual marketing term nobody's exaggerating. Furuzamami Beach is the one everyone photographs; Ama Beach, a short walk over, is quieter and just as good. Go as a day trip if you're short on time, but the diving is genuinely world-class, and humpback whales pass through in winter if your timing's different from mine.

![Furuzamami Beach on Zamami Island, a fifty-minute ferry from Naha — the water clarity here is the reason "Kerama Blue" is a real term](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6f/Furuzamami_beach_Okinawa_Zamami.jpg)

## Why Go Now

The math is specific: Shuri Castle's interior isn't open yet, the accommodation tax funding infrastructure upgrades hasn't fully kicked in, and the "budget Hawaii" narrative hasn't yet translated into the kind of pricing and crowding that narrative usually produces once it lands. JTA just launched Naha's first international route in February, direct flights from Taipei, Seoul, and Hong Kong keep multiplying, and none of it has caught up to itself yet. That's a short list of things that are all true at once and won't all still be true by next spring.

## The Honest Caveat

Junglia, the new theme park that opened in July 2025 with enormous hype, has been a rockier launch than advertised — mixed reviews, real traffic strain on roads that weren't built for it, and locals audibly annoyed about congestion and noise. If your itinerary includes it, budget extra time for getting there and don't expect the theme-park polish the marketing promised. And renting a car in Okinawa now means sitting in traffic you wouldn't have hit two years ago — the island's roads are absorbing tourism growth faster than they're being widened. None of that cancels out the trip. It just means the "undiscovered" window is closing in real time, not hypothetically.
