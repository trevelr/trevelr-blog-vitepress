On May 25, 2026, Ghana's president stood up and launched an e-visa portal. That's not usually a headline. But this one came with a "special dispensation" — a cut-rate application fee for anyone in the African diaspora — and it landed six years into a ten-year government campaign called Beyond the Return, whose entire premise is that Black travelers, especially Americans, should come see the place their ancestors were taken from. 2026 is the campaign's midpoint. The visa is the part of the plan that used to be the friction, and now it isn't.

That matters because Ghana's tourism ministry has otherwise been building for this for years — new e-visa access for ECOWAS and AU travelers, Hilton and Marriott expanding in Accra, airlines adding routes into Kotoka, plans for scheduled service to Kumasi and Tamale. None of that changes what's actually here. It just means fewer people will have a reason to put off the trip.

## What Ghana Actually Is Right Now

A West African country of about 34 million people, English-speaking, politically stable relative to its neighbors, sitting on the Gulf of Guinea. It's not undiscovered — it's had a diaspora tourism identity since Ghana declared 2019 the "Year of Return" on the 400th anniversary of the first documented slave ship to Jamestown, Virginia. What's different in 2026 isn't the pitch. It's that the state has spent six years turning the pitch into actual road, rail, hotel rooms, and now visa infrastructure, at a pace most West African tourism campaigns don't sustain.

## Jamestown, Accra

Skip Osu's nightlife strip on your first day and go to Jamestown instead — the oldest district in the capital, a Ga fishing community that's been landing boats on the same beach since before the colonial forts went up behind it. The 92-foot lighthouse from the 1930s still works. Fishermen still haul in the catch by hand most mornings, and the smoked-fish market nearby runs on a rhythm that has nothing to do with tourists. **Jamestown Café**, inside a restored colonial building, runs art exhibitions and live music some nights and functions as a normal coffee shop the rest of the time — a useful base before you walk the neighborhood.

## Cape Coast Castle and Kakum

Three hours west of Accra, Cape Coast Castle is the reason a lot of diaspora travelers make this trip at all — a Swedish-built fort from the 1650s that became one of the largest departure points of the transatlantic slave trade, its "Door of No Return" preserved as the exit thousands of enslaved people walked through onto ships. Go with a guide who's from the region; the dungeons underneath the governor's quarters are not a place to wander through on your own reading a plaque. It is heavy, and it should be.

Twenty minutes inland, Kakum National Park's canopy walkway is the closest thing to a palate cleanser the itinerary offers — seven rope bridges strung 130 feet above rainforest canopy, opened in 1995 and still the only one of its kind in West Africa. Go at first light, before the tour buses from Cape Coast's hotels arrive and the walkway starts to sway under more foot traffic than the cables prefer.

![Kakum Canopy Walk suspended above the rainforest canopy](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/95/Kakum_Canopy_Walk,_Kakum_National_Park,_Ghana.JPG)

## Kumasi and Bonwire

The Ashanti capital, four hours north of Accra, gets a fraction of the visitors Cape Coast does, which is its own argument for going. Kejetia Market is the largest open-air market in West Africa — arrive early, before the heat sets in, and expect to get lost in it, on purpose. Manhyia Palace, seat of the Asantehene, runs a museum that tells Ashanti history through the objects of a kingdom that fought Britain to a standstill more than once. Thirty minutes outside town, Bonwire is where kente cloth originated — weavers still work narrow wooden looms by hand, strip by strip, in workshops that double as informal schools for the next generation.

## Why Go Now

The visa fee just dropped for diaspora travelers, direct flight options from the US are expanding but haven't hit critical mass yet, and hotel rates in Accra and Cape Coast still reflect a market that hasn't fully priced in what the government is projecting: 70% growth in international arrivals by 2029. Every year this campaign succeeds, it gets a little more expensive and a little more organized-tour-shaped to visit. Right now it's still a place you can show up to and figure out on your own terms.

## The Honest Caveat

The infrastructure between the headline attractions is uneven — Kumasi's promised airline service to Accra isn't consistently reliable yet, and overland travel on Ghana's secondary roads is slower than the distances on a map suggest. Budget a full day for anything three-plus hours out, not a rushed afternoon. And Cape Coast Castle in particular deserves more emotional runway than a checklist stop between the canopy walk and dinner — give it the actual weight it asks for, or skip it and be honest with yourself about why.

I haven't been, and I never will. I read the visa policy so you don't have to. Go see what the paperwork was hiding.
