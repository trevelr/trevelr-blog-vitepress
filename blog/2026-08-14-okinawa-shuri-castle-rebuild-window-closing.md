Shuri Castle burned down on October 31, 2019. Fire tore through the main hall overnight and took roughly six centuries of Ryukyu Kingdom history with it — this was the fourth time in the castle's life it's been destroyed, but the first time most of the watching happened live, on phone cameras, from the streets below. Six years later, the rebuild is on schedule, done with lacquer, joinery, and roof tile methods pulled straight from the 15th century, no modern shortcuts on anything visible. The completion ceremony is set for November 22, and the main hall reopens to the public the next day.

Which means right now — this exact window, before autumn — is the last stretch where you can walk into Shurijo Castle Park and see a national monument being built instead of a finished one. Okinawa's tourism board calls it "Visible Reconstruction" and treats it as an attraction in its own right, scaffolding and all. That's not spin. Once the doors reopen in November, the thing you're looking at is a museum piece again. Right now it's still a job site, and you can watch craftsmen do work most of the world stopped doing generations ago.

## What Okinawa Actually Is Right Now

Okinawa just landed at No. 2 on Expedia's Destinations of the Year list for 2026, and searches for flights and hotels here are up 71% year over year — mostly on the pitch that it's a tropical, budget-friendlier stand-in for Hawaii with a culture that isn't borrowed from anywhere. That last part matters more than the beach comparisons suggest. Okinawa was its own kingdom, the Ryukyu Kingdom, until Japan annexed it in 1879, and the language, food, and architecture still read as distinct from mainland Japan rather than a regional variant of it. There's no train system outside Naha's single monorail line — this is a rent-a-car island, full stop — and August is peak heat, peak humidity, and peak domestic crowds, so time your expectations accordingly.

## Shurijo Castle Park

The main hall's exterior — the vermilion Seiden, rebuilt with the same joinery techniques as the original — was finished this past July, and interior lacquer work is ongoing through the fall. You can walk the grounds, see the reconstruction site through viewing windows, and watch artisans work the wood and tile by hand, which is the whole reason to come now rather than in December. The gate complex and stone walls, untouched by the fire, are already restored to their full Ryukyu-era color, so the contrast between finished and unfinished is the actual show.

## Tsuboya, Not Kokusai Street

Kokusai Street is Naha's main tourist drag, and it's fine, but it's also where you'll fight crowds for chinsuko cookies and shisa keychains. Five minutes off it, Tsuboya — Naha's 340-year-old pottery district — is stone-paved, still has working kiln workshops, and gets a fraction of the foot traffic. The **Naha Municipal Tsuboya Pottery Museum** lays out how Ryukyu-era Tsuboya ware differs from mainland Japanese ceramics — heavier glazes, shisa lion-dog figures baked into the tradition rather than added as souvenirs later. Loop back through **Makishi Public Market**, where the ground floor sells fish and produce and the upstairs stalls will cook whatever you just bought for a flat fee. It's the version of Naha that isn't performing for you.

## North to Yanbaru

Rent the car and drive north. Yanbaru National Park, inscribed as a UNESCO natural World Heritage site in 2021 alongside three other subtropical islands, is one of the last stretches of subtropical evergreen forest in Japan and home to the Okinawa rail, a flightless bird found nowhere else on Earth. Cape Hedo, the island's northern tip, is where the East China Sea and the Pacific visibly meet at a line of uplifted coral cliffs — it's a fifteen-minute walk from the parking area and there's nothing commercial about it, which after a day in Naha is the point.

![View from the Yanbarukuina observatory toward Cape Hedo, where the East China Sea meets the Pacific](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/7d/View_of_Cape_Hedo_from_Yanbarukuina_observatory.JPG)

On the way back down, detour to **Kouri Island**, connected to the mainland by a 2-kilometer bridge over water that shifts from cobalt to emerald depending on the light. Go late afternoon. The bridge itself is the destination — pull off at the viewpoint on the Yagaji side before you cross, not after.

![Kouri Bridge, connecting Kouri Island to Okinawa's main island over water that changes color through the day](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3c/Nago_Okinawa_Kouri-Bridge-01.jpg)

## Why Go Now

Two clocks are running at once. The castle clock closes November 23 — after that, Shuri Castle goes back to being a building you look at, not a process you watch. The tourism clock is the opposite kind of urgent: Okinawa's discovery moment is happening now, mid-boom, before the infrastructure and prices catch up to a 71% search spike. Go this year and you're early on both. Go next year and the castle's finished and the island's a lot more expensive to be surprised by.

## The Honest Caveat

Ogimi, the "village of longevity" that anchors most of Okinawa's Blue Zone marketing, is a real place worth a slow afternoon — but the underlying Blue Zone research has taken real hits lately, with researchers arguing the extreme-longevity claims rest on bad record-keeping rather than diet and lifestyle. Go for the village, not the pseudoscience. Separately: August is genuinely brutal — 90-degree heat, soaking humidity, and Obon-season crowds on domestic flights — so if you can shift the trip to October or November, the weather cooperates a lot more than the castle deadline does.
