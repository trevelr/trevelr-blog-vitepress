Set-jetting is supposedly an $8 billion industry now, and most of that money is chasing three places: Bath, for Bridgerton. The French Riviera and Taormina, for The White Lotus. Rome and Venice, for the new season of Emily in Paris. All glossy, all already overrun, all exactly where you'd expect a streaming-driven crowd to land.

Peaky Blinders: The Immortal Man came out on Netflix in March. Its most quoted shot — Cillian Murphy, as Tommy Shelby, crossing a narrowboat over open air on a canal 126 feet above a valley — was filmed on the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct in northeast Wales. It's been six months. Nobody's shown up.

That's the story. Not "here's a pretty canal." Here's a UNESCO World Heritage site that got a Netflix movie's best frame and still isn't on anyone's list.

## What It Actually Is

The Pontcysyllte Aqueduct carries the Llangollen Canal across the River Dee on eighteen stone piers, cast iron trough on top, finished in 1805 by Thomas Telford and William Jessop. It's the highest canal aqueduct in the world — 126 feet up, over a thousand feet long — and boats still cross it today, the same way they did before railways existed. You can walk across on the towpath for free, no railing on the canal side, which is the detail that makes people's stomachs drop in photos. Or you can pay Anglo Welsh ten pounds for their "Little Star" boat trip and do it the way Tommy Shelby did, minus the guns.

The town it belongs to, Llangollen, has about 3,400 people, a river gorge, a heritage steam railway that goes nowhere useful, and absolutely no idea it was supposed to be having a moment right now.

![The Pontcysyllte Aqueduct — eighteen piers, no railing on the canal side, boats still cross it daily](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bb/Pontcysyllte_Aqueduct.jpg)

## Four Places That Are Still Just for the Locals

**The aqueduct itself**, obviously — go at the ends of the day. Midday is fine but flat light; early morning or the hour before sunset is when the cast iron trough actually looks like something out of a period drama instead of infrastructure.

**Castell Dinas Bran**, the ruined 13th-century castle on the hill directly above town. It's a steep, unrelenting forty-five minutes up from the bridge in the town centre — no shade, no water source, bring your own — and the payoff is a 360-degree view of the Dee Valley plus a pile of stones that local legend ties to the Holy Grail. Nobody was up there when I'd expect a Netflix bump to have sent at least a few people with tripods.

![Castell Dinas Bran — a steep climb, a ruined castle, and a view of the whole valley](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fb/Castell_Dinas_Bran_-_geograph.org.uk_-_5396434.jpg)

**The Corn Mill**, a converted watermill pub built out over the rapids of the River Dee, with a deck that puts you directly above the white water. Order whatever's local — it's a gastropub doing seasonal Welsh menus, not a tourist trap that happens to have a good view — and sit outside if the weather allows it, which in this part of Wales is never guaranteed.

**Horseshoe Falls**, three miles upriver, a curved Telford-designed weir that feeds twelve million gallons a day into the canal system. It's not dramatic in the waterfall sense — more a long, elegant, engineered curve of white water — but it's the reason the whole canal exists, and almost nobody who does the aqueduct walk bothers to go see the thing that makes it work.

## Why Go Now

Two reasons, and they expire on different schedules. The film-tourism window is open precisely because it hasn't been discovered — the algorithm sent everyone to the Riviera and Bath instead, and there's no guarantee that stays true into 2027 once someone writes the "hidden Peaky Blinders location" listicle that's clearly coming. And separately, late September through October is the actual best window for the aqueduct walk on its own merits: narrowboat traffic thins out after the school-holiday season, the valley starts turning color, and you get the crossing without a queue of hire boats backed up behind you.

## The Honest Caveat

Llangollen's own railway station is heritage-only — it doesn't connect to the national network, so you're arriving by car or bus from Wrexham or Chirk, not by train, which undercuts the romance a little. The town itself is small enough that you'll have covered the center in an afternoon; this is a half-day anchor for a wider North Wales trip, not a destination you build a week around. And Welsh weather in autumn does what Welsh weather does — check the forecast and bring a shell layer regardless of what it says, because the valley makes its own microclimate and ignores the app.

None of that explains why a Netflix movie with a genuinely striking location shot produced zero visible bump. Maybe it will. Maybe six months just isn't enough runway compared to a show that's been building an audience for four seasons. Either way, right now, you'd have the aqueduct closer to yourself than any comparable set-jetting location in Europe.
