In 2019, Ghana invited the African diaspora home for one year, marking 400 years since the first enslaved Africans were forced onto ships bound for Virginia. The Year of Return was supposed to be a moment. Instead it became infrastructure. It's now "Beyond the Return: A Decade of African Renaissance," a ten-year government program running through 2030, and 2026 is the year the numbers stopped being a nice story and started being a strategy. Diaspora tourism is up roughly 30% this year alone. The country earned close to $4.8 billion from tourism last year and is on pace to beat it. Ghana's tourism ministry wants 70% growth in international arrivals by 2029, backed by a multi-billion-cedi development plan and new direct routes into Kotoka, Kumasi, and Tamale airports.

That's the tension worth sitting with. A pilgrimage that started as an invitation to grieve and reconnect is now a line item in a national growth target. Both things are true at once, and neither cancels the other out.

## What's Actually Happening

Ghana was always going to be a heavier trip than a beach week. Cape Coast and Elmina Castles, the two UNESCO-listed slave forts on the central coast, are where most diaspora visitors start and where the emotional weight of the trip concentrates. Diaspora travelers are spending an average of $3,742 a trip in 2026, and that money is pulling infrastructure with it — new hotels in Accra and Cape Coast, expanded flight capacity, a government tourism marketplace platform launched this year to coordinate bookings that used to run on word of mouth and diaspora Facebook groups. The volume is real. So is the strain on sites never designed to process this many people through a single narrow doorway.

## Cape Coast Castle

Start here, and go early. The Door of No Return — the exit through which enslaved people were marched onto ships — is a few feet wide, and it's where guided tours bottleneck hardest. Go with the first tour slot of the day if you can get it; the dungeons are unlit, low-ceilinged, and deliberately left that way, and a crowded group moving through them changes the experience from something you sit with to something you're shuffled past. This isn't a site to photograph quickly and move on from. Some tour operators are already treating it that way, and it shows in how rushed the mid-day groups feel compared to the morning ones.

## Kakum National Park

An hour inland from Cape Coast, and worth building into the same trip precisely because it's the emotional opposite of the castle. Seven rope-and-plank bridges strung between rainforest canopy trees, some over 300 years old, 27 meters up at the highest point. Go in the morning — the heat and the crowds both build by early afternoon, and the wildlife (hornbills, forest elephants if you're extremely lucky, mostly you'll hear more than you see) is more active before 9 a.m. It's not a detour from the heritage story. It's a reminder that this stretch of coast is also just a rainforest that predates all of it.

![The canopy walkway at Kakum National Park, suspended above the rainforest floor](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/95/Kakum_Canopy_Walk,_Kakum_National_Park,_Ghana.JPG)

## Jamestown, Accra

Skip Osu's Oxford Street strip for an afternoon and go to Jamestown instead — Accra's oldest neighborhood, rougher, poorer, and far more interesting than the diaspora-hotel version of the city. The 1871 lighthouse is still climbable, and the view from the top is the harbor, the colonial-era Ussher Fort, and a fishing community that's operated more or less the same way for a century. The chop bars here serve grilled tilapia with banku and shito that costs a fraction of what you'll pay in Osu and tastes better. If your trip lands in August, this is also where the Chale Wote Street Art Festival turns the neighborhood into an open-air gallery — check the dates before you book, because it reshapes the whole visit.

![Jamestown Lighthouse in Accra, built in 1871 and still in use](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b9/Jamestown_Lighthouse,_Accra.jpg)

## Bonwire, Near Kumasi

A few hours north, in the Ashanti Region, is the village where kente cloth originated and is still hand-woven on narrow double-heddle looms, strip by strip, exactly the way it's been done for centuries. This is a better way to spend an afternoon in Ashanti country than a rushed stop at Manhyia Palace: watch a weaver work an actual loom, ask what a pattern means (every one carries a specific historical or proverbial meaning — nothing is decorative for its own sake), and buy directly from the person who made it instead of a market stall in Accra reselling it at triple the price.

![A weaver working a traditional kente loom in the Ashanti Region](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e6/Weaving_of_kente_cloth.JPG)

## Why Go Now

Because the infrastructure catching up to the demand is genuinely new — more direct flights, a real hotel pipeline, a government platform trying to formalize a tourism economy that used to run on ad hoc diaspora networks — and none of it has fully hardened into the packaged, roped-off version of itself yet. You can still show up to Jamestown and have an unscripted conversation with someone on their porch. You can still get the first tour slot at Cape Coast Castle if you're willing to set an alarm. The government's own ten-year timeline runs through 2030, which means the version of this trip that exists right now, with the infrastructure half-built and the crowds not yet fully sorted into tour-bus lanes, is temporary by design.

## The Honest Caveat

The thing nobody tells you going in: the volume has started to change the texture of the experience it's built on. A site meant for quiet reckoning is increasingly run on tour-group scheduling, and some visitors report guides speed-running the darkest parts of the Cape Coast tour to hit their next group's slot. That's not a reason to skip it — the history doesn't get less true because the gift shop is bigger than it used to be. But go in knowing you're not visiting a static memorial. You're visiting a site in the middle of becoming an industry, and the version you get will depend heavily on what time you show up and how much you resist being moved along.
