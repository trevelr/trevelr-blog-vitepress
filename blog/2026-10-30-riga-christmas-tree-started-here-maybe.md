Every November the same four names get recommended for Christmas markets: Nuremberg, Strasbourg, Vienna, Cologne. They're good. They're also where everyone else is going.

Here's the contrarian pick, and it comes with a fight attached. Riga claims the first public Christmas tree. Tallinn says no.

## The claim, and the catch

A stone plaque set into the cobbles of Town Hall Square, near the House of the Blackheads, marks the spot of a tree dated to 1510. It was placed in 2010, five hundred years on, and carries its message in eight languages. The story rests on the records of the Brotherhood of Blackheads, a guild of unmarried merchants, who, by Riga's telling, decorated a tree with artificial roses in the marketplace, danced around it, and then burned it.

Tallinn points to its own Blackheads and to documents from 1441 and 1442 mentioning a tree on 25 December. Both cities agree the guild started it. They disagree on which city's guild did it first.

I can't settle that, and neither can anyone who wasn't there. But a city that holds a grudge over a tree has a certain commitment to the bit. That's worth something in a market town.

## Where to actually spend your time

The official market runs in the Old Town, centered on Dome Square beside Riga Cathedral, the largest medieval church in the Baltics. Expect it to open November 27 and run into early January. Sources differ on the end date (January 3 or 4), and the city hadn't published its 2026 listing when I checked, so confirm before booking flights.

![Riga Cathedral, anchoring Dome Square](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/80/Riga_-_Dome_Cathedral.jpg)

**Esplanāde Park.** The smaller market sits in front of the Orthodox Cathedral, outside the medieval tangle. It's the one to try if Dome Square is shoulder-to-shoulder. Different crowd, more room to stand still with a hot drink.

![The Christmas market at Esplanāde Park](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/78/Christmas_market_at_Esplan%C4%81de_park_in_Riga.jpg)

**Riga Central Market.** This isn't festive, which is the point. Five pavilions built between 1924 and 1930 out of German Zeppelin hangars, moved from the Vaiņode air base in western Latvia. It's one of the largest markets in Europe and part of the UNESCO-listed historic centre. Go on a weekday morning, when people are buying dinner rather than photographing it. Cold weather and a covered market is the right pairing.

![Riga Central Market pavilions](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b4/Riga_Central_Market.02.jpg)

**Alberta iela.** Walk it after dark when the facades are lit and the street is empty. It's lined with Art Nouveau work by Mikhail Eisenstein, and at number 12 the Riga Art Nouveau Museum sits in the former home of architect Konstantīns Pēkšēns. Buildings on this street get misattributed constantly online, so trust the plaques over the blog posts. Including this one.

![Art Nouveau facade near Dome Square](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/30/Dome_square_in_Riga_-_Art_Nouveau_building.jpg)

## Why go now

Winter travel trend reports this year keep landing on the same advice: quieter destinations, off-season dates, cooler weather as a feature instead of a flaw. Riga fits all three. It has a medieval core, one of the densest Art Nouveau districts anywhere, and a market that hasn't turned into a queue.

The timing argument is simple. The big German markets are priced and packed for the season. Riga gives you the same mulled-wine-and-wooden-stalls setup with a medieval skyline and a historical feud thrown in.

## The caveat

It gets dark around 4 p.m. in December, and it's properly cold. Latvian winters aren't atmospheric, they're a commitment. Bring real boots. The Old Town cobbles are also unforgiving in ice.

And the "first tree" claim is a contested tourism line, not settled history. Enjoy it as one.
