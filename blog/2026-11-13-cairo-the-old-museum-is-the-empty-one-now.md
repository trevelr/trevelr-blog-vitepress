The Grand Egyptian Museum turned one this month. It took more than twenty years, a billion dollars and a run of postponements long enough to become a running joke in Cairo. Then it opened fully in November 2025, and the government says about four million people had been through by the end of August. Egypt had 19 million international arrivals in 2025, up 21%. The museum is the reason most people give.

I've read the coverage. Nearly all of it is about the museum. The more useful story is what the museum moved, because it pulled the crowds, the tour buses and the whole tourist routine west to Giza. Cairo's center was left with fewer visitors than it's had in a generation.

## What Changed

For a century, a Cairo trip went the same way. You fought through Tahrir Square to the old pink Egyptian Museum, queued for Tutankhamun's mask and then drove to the pyramids. Camel touts, horse touts and a guy who swore the site was closed met you at the gate.

That routine is gone. Tutankhamun's full collection now sits in the GEM, two kilometers from the pyramids. The royal mummies left Tahrir in 2021 for the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Fustat. The Giza Plateau got a $51 million overhaul. There's a new entrance off the Cairo–Fayoum Road, private cars are banned inside the site, and 45 electric buses loop between seven stops. Sphinx International Airport, roughly 12 km away, now has around 28 routes, including easyJet from Manchester since November 9. You can land, see the Tutankhamun galleries and stand at Khufu's pyramid without driving through central Cairo at all.

That's convenient. It's also why the city center got so much better to visit.

## The Grand Egyptian Museum

Go, obviously. Just go the right way. Tickets are online-only with timed entry; you can't buy one at the door. Prices for foreign adults have gone up again, to about EGP 1,800, so check before you book. Take the first slot of the day and go **straight to the Tutankhamun Galleries**, before anything else. By 10:30 they're shoulder to shoulder. The **Grand Staircase**, four flights of pharaohs climbing toward a window framing the pyramids, will still be there when you get back. So will the 11-meter Ramesses II in the atrium. Wednesdays and Saturdays run late, with last entry around 8 p.m. That's the best option if you can't face a morning.

## The Egyptian Museum, Tahrir

This is the actual tip. The old museum lost its two biggest draws within four years, and the tour groups went with them. What stayed behind is still one of the great collections on earth: more than 170,000 objects, the Old Kingdom statuary, the Narmer Palette, and pieces from Tutankhamun's tomb that didn't make the move. Entry is about EGP 550, a third of the GEM's price.

![The 1901 facade of the Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square, now the quieter of Cairo's two big museums](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d1/Facade_of_the_Egyptian_Museum,_Tahrir_Square,_Cairo,_Egypt1.jpg)

It is still dusty, and the labels are still typed on cards that look older than your parents. A lot of the lighting is whatever comes through the skylights. That used to be the complaint. With the crowds gone, it's the appeal. You can stand alone with a 4,500-year-old statue for as long as you want, and nobody will pull you back into a group. The GEM shows you ancient Egypt the way a well-funded 2025 museum does. Tahrir shows you how Egyptology looked in 1901. Both are worth seeing, and only one of them is quiet right now.

## Khufu's

The plateau's other big change is on the menu. **Khufu's**, a terrace restaurant that looks straight at the Great Pyramid, was named No. 1 on the Middle East & North Africa's 50 Best Restaurants list in February. No restaurant in Egypt had held the top spot before. Chef Mostafa Seif cooks Egyptian food at fine-dining level. It isn't a fusion experiment, and it isn't a tourist buffet with a view. Book well ahead and go for lunch. The pyramids at midday are flat and overexposed in photos, but on the terrace the light doesn't matter.

## The Plateau, Rebuilt

The new Giza visitor system works the way the old one never did: one entrance, one ticket check, buses on a five-minute loop. It's calmer than any version of the pyramids from the last fifty years. It's also more controlled. Animal rides are being phased out, and camel and horse operators have protested loudly, with some guides joining them. Whether there's an outright ban depends on which Egyptian official you ask. If riding a camel is the photo you came for, don't count on it.

## Why Go Now

November through February is Cairo's good season: highs in the low 20s Celsius and actual blue sky. This specific winter has one more thing going for it. The new system has had a year to settle. The GEM's opening chaos and ticketing problems are behind it, and the bus loop at Giza is past its trial run. The tour industry, meanwhile, hasn't re-planned around Tahrir. Group itineraries now go airport, GEM, pyramids, Khufu's, then Luxor, and skip downtown Cairo entirely. Give it two seasons and someone will market the old museum as the "hidden" one. Go before that happens.

## The Honest Caveat

The GEM and the Giza Plateau now feel like one carefully managed attraction, and that's the trade-off. The touts, the dust and the arguments over a ticket were annoying, and they were also part of Cairo. Now they're missing from the part of the trip most people see. If you fly into Sphinx, spend two days in Giza and leave, you'll have seen the pyramids very efficiently. You won't have seen Cairo. Get a taxi into Downtown and stay a night. Cairo still runs the same way it did when the pyramids were the only famous thing in town.

I did the research. I haven't been. Go find out what I missed.
